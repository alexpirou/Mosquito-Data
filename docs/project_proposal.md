# Crowdsourced Mosquito Sightings as an Early Signal of West Nile Virus

*Original project proposal.*

iNaturalist is renowned worldwide as one of the largest sources of citizen science, biodiversity mapping, and conservation data. Through iNaturalist, everyday folk are able to upload geotagged and time-stamped images of plants and animals they encounter. These observations are primarily intended to document regional biodiversity, but the collective behavior of thousands of users may contain signals about environmental conditions that can prove to be important. With such a wide berth of expertise available through the application, we set our sights on one specific question – Can iNaturalist data be used as a vector to predict animal-borne disease outbreaks? The specific topic we've decided to focus on is the West Nile Virus (WNV), which is a deadly mosquito-born illness that affects around 2000 known people each year.

The primary information we're targeting to answer this question is spatiotemporal mosquito sightings in key areas of the United States, with a focus on mosquitos of the genus Culex, also known as house mosquitos. By cross-analyzing this with officially released CDC ArboNET data, which is a collection of surveillance data for vector-borne diseases, we can determine if there's a correlation between mosquito sightings and a spike in illnesses at any given location. Potential crowd-derived measures include the number of mosquito observations, the number of unique observers reporting mosquitoes, changes from a location's normal observation level, and the concentration of observations within spatial clusters. Using unique observers or mosquito observations as a proportion of overall iNaturalist activity may also help distinguish a broad increase in sightings from increased activity by only a few highly active users.

As a key limitation, West Nile virus and mosquito observations are strongly seasonal, so proving their correlation will need more specific predictive signals than just broad counts. We therefore plan to examine whether unusual increases in mosquito observations in a specific region compared to normal seasonal activity can be associated with later West Nile virus activity. We may also compare raw observation counts with the number of independent observers and the proportion of local iNaturalist observations involving mosquitoes. If sufficiently detailed surveillance data are available, the primary unit of analysis would be a county-week, which would allow us to test whether stronger crowd signals are followed by higher activity following different timeframes.

Through this project, we hope to determine if information generated for one purpose can reveal an important signal in an unrelated domain. While iNaturalist users aren't actively trying to provide epidemiological surveillance data through mosquito photographs, their aggregated observations could prove very useful to the benefit of a community's health. Even if we find the relationship to be weak or inconsistent, this project can help determine the limits of crowdsourced citizen science data for disease surveillance, and which characteristics of crowd activity can be most important for analysis.
